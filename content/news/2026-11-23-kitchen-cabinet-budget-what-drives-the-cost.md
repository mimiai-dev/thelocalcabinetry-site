A kitchen cabinet budget is driven mostly by how many cabinets you need, not by the size of the room alone. Linear footage sets the starting point. Cabinet count, door style, drawer banks, extras, delivery and installation then move the total up or down. Build the budget from a cabinet list, and add a cushion for surprises.

We don't quote prices in articles. The price list is the same for everyone, and you can read more on our [cost guide](/cost-guide/). This page covers what pushes the number around, so you can plan before you order.

## Start with linear feet and cabinet count

Linear feet is the total run of cabinets along your walls. Bob Vila's cost guide names it, along with the kitchen footprint, as a main factor in how many units you replace. It is a good first measure, but it is not the whole story.

Two kitchens with the same linear footage can need different numbers of cabinets. A wall of 90 inches could be three 30 inch cabinets or six 15 inch ones. Labor for installation is often billed per cabinet, so a higher count can raise that part of the bill. Fewer, wider cabinets are often simpler to plan, though your layout and what you store decide what works.

Count every box, not just the long runs:

- Base cabinets
- Wall cabinets
- Tall pantry or oven cabinets
- Corner units
- Any small cabinets that fill odd gaps

## Door style, finish and drawers

Door style changes the cost. A flat slab door and a door with a recessed panel are built differently, and custom or painted finishes usually cost more than standard ones. Bob Vila notes that painting adds material and labor cost.

Construction type matters too. The same guide says framed cabinets usually cost less than frameless, with savings of up to about 15 percent. Check which type a layout is drawn in before you compare numbers.

Drawers versus doors is the other big choice. A drawer bank needs drawer boxes and slides, while a door cabinet needs hinges and a shelf. Bob Vila lists extra drawer banks among the features that add cost, along with pull-out shelves and lazy Susans. Many people want drawers in the base cabinets because they are easier to use. Decide where they matter most, such as pots and pans, and keep simple doors elsewhere.

Hardware deserves a line of its own. The same guide calls it a surprising expense. Pulls and knobs are counted per piece, and a kitchen can need dozens. Count the doors and drawers, then count the pulls.

## Fillers, options and the small parts

Fillers are narrow strips that close the gap between a cabinet and a wall or between two cabinets. Fine Homebuilding explains why they come up. Stock cabinets come in set widths, usually in 3 inch steps, so when your wall does not match, a filler makes up the difference. Many walls will need at least a couple.

Options add up the same way. Fine Homebuilding points out that in semi-custom lines every option and upgrade is added to the base price. That is true of most cabinet lines, so list the extras you want before you compare totals.

Trim pieces, end panels and toe kicks finish the look. They are small items, and they are easy to forget on a first budget. Our design drawing lists them, so they show up before you order and not after.

## Delivery and installation

Plan time as well as money. Our lead time is about 7 days. Shipping within the US then takes 3 to 7 days, depending on your location. For custom work we confirm the production time when the order is placed. We ship from Houston and U.S. warehouses.

Our cabinets are RTA, ready to assemble. We do not assemble or install them. Your budget needs a line for assembly and installation, whether you do the work yourself or hire someone. Fine Homebuilding notes that many RTA cabinets need professional assembly, which adds labor cost. Bob Vila points out that installation labor, hauling away old cabinets, and moving appliances or utilities all add to the total. Ask your installer how they bill, per cabinet or by the job, before you finalize the layout.

## How to plan the budget

1. Set a ceiling for the whole kitchen first. Bob Vila's planning guide suggests setting a maximum early.
2. Expect cabinets and hardware to take the largest slice. That guide puts it at about 40 percent of a kitchen budget. Other sources give lower shares, so treat it as a rough guide.
3. Hold back a cushion. The same guide says to leave room for repairs that turn up once walls are opened.
4. List cabinets by type and count, then add doors, drawers and pulls.
5. Add fillers, trim, delivery and installation as separate lines.
6. Compare options with the same construction and door style, so the numbers mean the same thing.

If the total is too high, cut in this order: extras like pull-outs, then the number of drawer banks, then the finish. Cutting cabinet count usually means changing the layout, so do that on the drawing and not on the order.
