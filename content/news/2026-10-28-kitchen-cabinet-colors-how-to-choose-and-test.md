The best way to choose kitchen cabinet colors is to start with what you cannot change, which is your floor, your counters and the light in the room. Pick a color that shares an undertone with those, then test a large sample in the room for a few days. A small chip under store lights tells you very little.

Here is the order we suggest.

## Start with the floor and counters

Floors and counters are the biggest fixed surfaces in most kitchens. Cabinets should agree with them, not fight them.

Look at the undertone first. Every color leans warm (yellow, red, cream) or cool (blue, green, gray). A warm oak floor next to a cool, bluish white cabinet can look off. A warm white or a soft greige usually sits better. If the floor is cool gray tile, a crisp white or a gray-blue cabinet will feel more settled.

A few simple rules:

- Pick one family, warm or cool, and keep most surfaces in it.
- If the counter or floor has a strong pattern, keep the cabinet color quiet.
- If the counter and floor are plain, you have room for a stronger color like deep blue or green.
- Dark cabinets usually look better with a lighter or mid-tone floor. Light cabinets can handle a darker floor.

Do not forget the hardware finish. Brass, black and nickel each pull the eye toward a different undertone.

## Read the light in your room

Light changes color more than most people expect. Two things matter: which way the room faces, and what bulbs you use.

North-facing rooms get cooler, dimmer light. Warmer colors tend to help there, and a very dark cabinet can look even darker. South-facing rooms get warmer, brighter light, so lighter colors and cooler hues both work well. East and west rooms shift during the day, so check them morning and evening.

Bulbs matter too. Warm bulbs bring out yellow in a color. Cool bulbs bring out blue and gray. Try your samples under the bulbs you plan to keep, not only in daylight.

You may see a number called LRV on paint cards. It means light reflectance value, a scale from 0 (absorbs all light) to 100 (reflects all light). Colors below about 40 feel darker and cozier. Colors above 60 feel brighter. If your kitchen is dim, a higher number can help. Treat it as a guide, not a rule.

## Judge samples the right way

Most color mistakes happen at the sample step. These habits help:

1. **Go big.** Use a sample at least 1 by 1 foot. Bigger is better. A color that looks calm on a chip can feel strong across a whole run of cabinets.
2. **Look at one color at a time.** Cover the other swatches on the card so they do not change what you see.
3. **Use a board you can move.** Two coats on poster board lets you carry it around. It also avoids the old wall color bending what you see.
4. **Leave it up for a couple of days.** Check it in morning, midday and evening, in daylight and with the lights on.
5. **Set it beside the real things.** Hold it against your floor sample, your counter sample and your wall color.
6. **Let paint samples dry.** Dry paint looks darker than wet paint, so do not judge a color while it is still wet.
7. **Stand back.** Look from the doorway, since that is how you will see the kitchen most days.

Narrow the list to two or three colors before you test. More than that and the differences blur.

## Finish and door style change the look

The same color reads differently depending on the door. A flat door shows color evenly. A shaker door adds shadow lines, so the color looks a little deeper at the edges. Wood-grain finishes add their own warmth, so check them against your floor the same way.
