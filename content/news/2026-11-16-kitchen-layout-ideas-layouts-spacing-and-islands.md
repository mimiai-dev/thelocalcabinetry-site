The kitchen layout ideas that work for most homes are one-wall, galley, L-shaped and U-shaped, with an island added only if the room is big enough. Keep 42 inches between facing counters for one cook, and 48 inches if two people cook. A 3 by 5 foot island with 42 inches of clearance on every side needs about 120 square feet. If your room is smaller, a peninsula is usually the safer choice.

Below we cover each layout, the spacing numbers, corner options and how to test an island on paper.

## The common kitchen layouts

**One-wall.** All cabinets and appliances sit on a single wall. It suits small rooms and open plans. The downside is limited counter space and long walks between the sink and stove.

**Galley.** Two parallel runs of cabinets. This Old House suggests putting the sink and fridge on one wall and the range on the opposite wall. It works well in narrow rooms because everything is within a step or two.

**L-shaped.** Cabinets on two adjoining walls. It leaves the rest of the room open for a table or an island, and it fits many room shapes.

**U-shaped.** Cabinets on three walls. You get the most counter and storage, and traffic stays out of the cooking area. The room needs enough width so the aisles do not feel tight.

**Peninsula.** A run of cabinets that sticks out from a wall. It gives you extra counter and a spot to sit, like an island, but it only needs walkway on the open sides.

## Work-zone spacing

The old rule is the work triangle: sink, stove and refrigerator. This Old House puts the three sides at a combined 13 to 26 feet. That is a guide, not a code rule. Use it as a quick check, then plan around how you actually cook.

The spacing numbers we use most, from Fine Homebuilding's summary of NKBA guidelines:

- Work aisle, one cook: about 42 inches.
- Work aisle, two or more cooks: about 48 inches.
- Walkway where people only pass through: about 36 inches.
- Measure aisles from the front of one counter, tall cabinet or appliance to the front of the next.

Allow for open doors too. Doors and drawers should not interfere with each other across an aisle. If a dishwasher faces a fridge or oven, go wider than the minimum and check how far each door swings.

A simple way to think about zones:

1. **Prep.** Counter space next to the sink and within reach of the stove.
2. **Cook.** Stove or cooktop, with counter on at least one side.
3. **Clean.** Sink and dishwasher, with trash and recycling close.
4. **Store.** Fridge and pantry, near the entry so others can reach them without crossing the cook.

## Corner solutions

Corners waste space if you leave them as a plain box. You have three common options.

- **Blind corner cabinet.** One cabinet runs into the corner and the next one hides part of it. You need pull-out hardware to reach the back.
- **Lazy Susan.** Rotating shelves in a corner base. Good for bowls, pots and large items.
- **Corner drawers.** KraftMaid describes a corner base with three drawers that slide out and takes the place of a lazy Susan. It makes the corner reachable without turning shelves.

Pick based on what you store. Pots and big bowls do well on rotating shelves. Pans and lids do well in deep drawers. Whatever you choose, check that the doors and drawers in both runs can open without hitting each other at the corner.

## When an island fits

Do the math before you fall for the picture. From Fine Homebuilding:

- An island narrower than 24 inches cannot hold a sink, cooktop or dishwasher.
- Keep it under about 5 feet wide, or things get stranded in the middle.
- Plan about 42 inches of clearance on all sides. A 3 by 5 foot island with that clearance needs roughly 120 square feet.
- Walkways around an island can drop to 36 inches if you are tight. Check that doors can still open.

If you want stools, add more room. Fine Homebuilding gives these figures for seating:

- About 12 inches of overhang at a 42 inch counter, and about 15 inches at a 36 inch counter.
- At least 2 feet of counter length per seat.
- About 36 inches from the counter edge to the nearest obstruction if people only shuffle behind the stools, and about 44 inches if others walk past.

If the numbers do not work, try a peninsula, a smaller island, or a rolling cart. Tape the island outline on the floor and cook a meal around it. If you keep bumping it, it is too big.
