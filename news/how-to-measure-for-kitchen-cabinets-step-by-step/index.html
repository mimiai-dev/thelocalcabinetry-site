How to measure for kitchen cabinets, step by step

Here is how to measure for kitchen cabinets: pick one corner, work around the room in one direction, and write down every wall length, every opening, and the center of every outlet and pipe. Measure each wall at more than one height. Then check the walls and corners for square and plumb. That gives you a drawing you can trust before you order anything.

You need a steel tape, a pencil, graph paper or a notes app, a 4-foot level, and a helper for long walls. Plan on about an hour for an average kitchen. Rushing is where most fit problems begin.

Start with a sketch and the wall lengths

Draw the room from above, roughly to shape. It does not need to be neat. Label the walls 1, 2, 3 and so on, going clockwise from a starting corner. Keep that order for every measurement you take.

For each wall:

  • Measure the full length, corner to corner.
  • Measure it low, near countertop height, and again high, near the ceiling. Walls are often not straight, and the two numbers can differ.
  • Write down the smaller number if they differ. A cabinet run that fits the short number will fit.
  • Measure ceiling height at each corner and at the middle of each wall.

Write measurements in inches, and use fractions down to 1/8 inch. Do not round to the nearest foot.

Check that walls and corners are square and plumb

Cabinets are square boxes. Old walls usually are not. Hold a level against each wall, check the corners with a framing square, and lay a long straight board along the wall to spot bows. This Old House uses the same checks in its cabinet install guide.

Note any corner that is clearly off, and note where the wall bows. An installer can shim and scribe small gaps. A gap you did not plan for is what turns into a cabinet that will not slide into place.

Check the floor too. Floors are rarely level. This Old House has you mark a level line about 48 inches up the wall, then measure down to the floor in several spots. The shortest measurement marks the high point of the floor. Base cabinets are laid out from there, with the top of the base at 34 inches above that point. Write your high spot on the sketch.

Windows and doors

For each window, record:

  • The distance from the nearest corner to the outside edge of the trim on each side.
  • The width and height of the trim, not just the glass.
  • The sill height from the floor.
  • The trim or apron below the sill.

Trim is the detail people miss. Family Handyman notes that standard wall cabinets are 12 inches deep and hang about 54 inches from the floor. A window with a low sill or wide casing can collide with a cabinet or a countertop. Know where it sits before you pick sizes.

For each door or opening, record:

  • The distance from the corner to the trim on both sides.
  • The width of the opening, including trim.
  • Which way the door swings, and how far.

A door that swings into a cabinet face is a common surprise. Draw the swing arc on your sketch.

Plumbing, outlets and everything else on the wall

Measure from your starting corner to the center of each item. Also write down its height from the floor. Do this for:

  • The sink drain and both water supply lines.
  • The dishwasher supply and drain.
  • Gas lines, range outlets and hood vents.
  • Every outlet and switch.
  • The thermostat, vents, and air returns in the floor or wall.
  • The refrigerator water line, if there is one.

Do not assume the drain sits where the sink is. Pipes can run to one side or come out of the floor. Look inside the existing sink cabinet and measure what you see.

Outlets need a second look. Fine Homebuilding explains that the National Electrical Code asks for a countertop receptacle within 24 inches of the end of each counter run, and no more than 48 inches between them. Codes vary by town, so check yours. If a cabinet will cover an existing outlet, plan where it moves. Do that before the cabinets arrive.

Also mark stud locations if you can. Measure from the corner to the center of each stud. It helps you plan where cabinets get fastened.

Mistakes that cause a cabinet not to fit

These are the common ones:

  1. Measuring once. Walls differ top to bottom. Take two numbers.
  2. Measuring in feet and rounding. An inch lost across five cabinets is a gap or a jam.
  3. Forgetting trim. Window and door casing takes real space. Measure to the outside of it.
  4. Skipping the swing. Doors, appliance doors and drawers all need room to open.
  5. Ignoring depth. Base cabinets are usually 24 inches deep. Family Handyman gives 34-1/2 inches for the box height, or 36 inches with a countertop. Check that the room allows both.
  6. Putting a tall cabinet next to a tight corner. Corner runs need room for doors and drawers to clear each other. Plan filler space on paper.
  7. Not allowing for fillers. Filler strips cover small gaps against walls. Measure the gap at top and bottom, since it often differs.
  8. Moving plumbing or outlets in your head. If a pipe is behind a cabinet, the cabinet needs a cutout. Mark it on the plan.

Double-check before you send anything

Add up your wall measurements against the cabinet run. If the cabinets add up to more than the wall, something has to change. If they add up to less, the difference is filler or trim. Then walk the room once more with your sketch and confirm each number against the tape.

Take photos of each wall, straight on, with the tape visible if you can. They help us catch what a drawing misses.
